# Manifesto

## Introduction

Generative AI is faster at producing answers than humans are at thinking them through. When generating becomes cheaper than thinking, the path of least resistance takes over: the AI proposes, the human accepts, and speed starts replacing thought.

That shift creates risks that are easy to miss:

- **Judgment drifts away from the person.** The faster the machine can produce, the easier it becomes to stop questioning, exploring and deciding deliberately.
- **Accountability dissolves.** Work goes out that nobody fully understands — code, a report, a post. When it fails, "the AI wrote it" explains nothing — a machine cannot own an outcome.
- **Understanding erodes.** Tests pass, drafts go unread, and people sign off on what they cannot explain.
- **Design erodes.** AI is good at producing locally plausible pieces, but local plausibility is not global coherence. Without someone holding the intent, structure starts being accumulated rather than designed.
- **Plausibility replaces value.** Generated work often looks complete and correct, but looking right is not the same as being useful, true or worth making.
- **Existing controls weaken.** Reviews, roles and definitions of done still matter, but they need to evolve when machines perform a significant part of the work.

None of this is an argument against AI. **It is an argument against surrendering judgment to speed.**

AI should make production cheaper, not thinking optional.

The question is not whether AI produces the work. It is who remains in charge of it.

## What we hold

Talaia stands on a simple conviction: the human person comes first,
and no tool can take the place of human judgment or responsibility.

Two kinds of tower say it better than we can.[^4] Babel: one tower,
one direction, one language for everyone, built upward in the belief
that scale itself was progress — and when it fell, no one could
understand anyone, or explain what had been built. And the *talaies*[^5]:
the low watchtowers that line the Mediterranean coast. No one raised
them from above; each one was built by a village to watch its own
stretch of sea. Inside each there is a person who answers for what
they see, and the alarm passes from tower to tower by fire. The
choice in front of us is not "yes" or "no" to the technology; it is
which of the two towers we are building: Babel — one tower, all
scale, no one accountable — or the *talaies* — many small towers,
each one watched. The watchtower does not hold back the sea. Someone
in it keeps watch, and answers for what passes.

Keeping that order, we have
come to value:[^1]

**Human judgment** over machine autonomy  
**A person who answers** over a system with no owner  
**Understanding what goes out** over accepting what works  
**Deliberate design** over generated accumulation  
**Knowing when not to use it** over using it because it is there  
**Work that serves people** over work that merely looks complete

A tool serves best when it strengthens the
person who works and fails the moment it invites that person to stop
being present.

Talaia adheres in full to
[The Pro-Human AI Declaration](https://humanstatement.org/), which
also inspired it.

## What AI is

At Talaia we know that AI is software, and only software. It is not
alive: it has no intelligence or perception comparable to a
person's, no spirit, no desires of its own. It does not understand
what it produces. It imitates language, analysis, even empathy and
care, and the imitation can be useful; but its learning is
statistical adjustment over data, not the growth of someone shaped
by choices, mistakes and time. And it is not neutral: its answers
carry the choices and assumptions of the few people who designed
and trained it, so what sounds like a fact may be their opinion.

That makes Talaia more than a proposal for how to work with AI. It
is a defense of human work with AI: against the ways of working that
dilute responsibility, and against replacing human grace, talent and
personality. AI should augment our intelligence, through the tool,
not replace it. We have much more to bring to this world.

We call the principle that follows from this **human-in-charge**.


# Human-in-charge

## The principle

Human-in-charge means that every use of AI has a person in charge of
it: someone who decided it should exist, designed how it works,
understands it, and answers for it by name. It is not a human placed
in the path of a system to approve each thing the system produces.
It is a person who stands behind the system itself.

Take a process that uses AI to classify a thousand emails a day.
Nobody needs to read, check and understand a thousand
classifications; that would undo the reason for building it. What
must be true is that a person can say what the process is for and
what value it brings, how it is built and where the AI sits in it,
what can go wrong and what happens when it does, and that the
outcome is theirs. The machine may act a thousand times with no one
watching. It never acts on its own authority: it acts on the
authority of the person who put it there, inside the limits that
person drew.

Being in charge is held at three levels, and none of them is the
output of an automation:

- **Responsibility.** A named person answers for what the system
  does, including what it does when no one is looking.
- **Design.** A person decided what is handed to the machine and
  what is not, knowing the risks and the value. The structure was
  designed, not accumulated.
- **Understanding.** A person understands the code and the structure
  that carry the AI, and can explain how they work and why without
  the help of an AI.

We hold this as a belief, not a proposal. The harm AI can do is
avoided only when **every** use of AI has a human in charge of it,
without exception, as an obligation and not a recommendation. 

## How to apply it

Responsibility is not taken from a person: it is given up a little at a time, one unexamined
delegation after another, until none is left. Each step is
comfortable. The system quietly shapes how people work and judge, it
is accepted until it is no longer seen, and what was built comes to
look inevitable.[^6] What is lost on the way is cognitive
sovereignty: a person's own judgment. 
The defence is friction:

- **Stop and ask** who decided that this should be handed to the
  machine, and by what measure its answers count as correct.
- **Keep deciding, deliberating and doubting**, even though thinking
  is tiring and the machine is not.
- **Follow no oracle.** No guru, model or algorithm can carry the
  responsibility of choosing.
- **Do not delegate what cannot be optimised** or reduced to a
  calculation.

Talaia makes this practicable by bringing the manifesto and the
Certificate of Understanding into every piece of work where AI is
used. The manifesto is the frame: why the person stands above the
tool. The certificate is the practice, and it is signed over what a
person makes — the code, the structure, the design of the
automation — not over each thing the automation later produces.
Where there is no name, nobody is in charge.


# The Certificate of Understanding

## The Certificate

The Certificate of Understanding is a tool for applying the manifesto
day to day, for people who work with AI. Its statements aim to sum up
the actionable part of the manifesto.

The text, in the shape of the DCO:[^2]

```txt
Certificate of Understanding
Version 1 — talaia.dev/certificate

By signing off on this work, I certify that:

(a) The work was created, in whole or in part, with the
    assistance of an AI, at my direction and to a brief
    that was mine; and

(b) I have read the work in full; I understand what it
    says and why, and can explain it without the help of
    an AI; I have checked what it claims, and its
    opinions and assumptions are mine; and I take
    responsibility for the work and make it my own.

I sign this in keeping with the talaia manifesto: talaia.dev
```

Two clauses, and the signature means both at once: the machine was
here, and a person answers for what it made.[^3]

## Against Co-Authorship

The certificate records assistance, never co-authorship. An author is not the one who produced the
words but the one who stands behind them.  To author is to mean something and to be held to it. That takes a self with something at stake, and a machine has none.

Some tools say otherwise. Claude Code, by default, ends the commits
it writes with a git trailer that names the machine as an author:

```text
Co-Authored-By: Claude <noreply@anthropic.com>
```

We are against that use and against that definition. The trailer is
not just a label: GitHub and other forges read it and list the
machine next to the person, as an author of the change. Calling it a co-author turns help into authorship,
and splits the responsibility between a person and something that
cannot answer for anything. Talaia keeps the two apart:
`Assisted-by:` names the machine that helped, `Signed-off-by:` names
the person who answers.

## How to use it

The text lives at [talaia.dev/certificate](https://talaia.dev/certificate)
and is never signed there. You sign by adding one line to the work:

```text
talaia.dev/certificate — John Doe
```

Two parts, in that order:

- **The address** is the certificate. It points at a fixed text, so
  the line means the same thing wherever it appears, and clause (a)
  is already in it: an AI was here.
- **The name** is the certification. A person is here, and both
  clauses are true of them.

Adding the line *is* the act; the gesture is deliberate, and it is
yours.

Under a post, the line as it is. At the foot of a report, the
address once and one name per hand, because the work passed from
hand to hand — the analyst to the head of finance, the head of
finance to the board — and each hand adds its own name under the
last:

```text
talaia.dev/certificate
John Doe, Head of Finance, 2026-09-14
Jane Roe, CEO, 2026-09-16
```

In a commit, the address goes in the message and `-s` writes the
name as the `Signed-off-by:` trailer; the reviewer's sign-off on the
pull request is the next hand:

```sh
git commit -s -m "feat: export orders as CSV (#42)" -m "talaia.dev/certificate"
```

```text
feat: export orders as CSV (#42)

talaia.dev/certificate

Signed-off-by: John Doe <john@example.com>
```

The lines accumulate, and every one of them is a person who read the
work in full and answers for it. That is the chain of towers: the
fire passes, and at every tower someone is watching.

# Talaia

The manifesto and the certificate are the same for everyone. What
changes is who adopts them, and there are two ways in: a person or a
team, on their own; or a company, as a whole.

Talaia is open and free to use, and nobody's permission is needed.
You already have the certificate. What remains is the way of working

the AI, how to read what comes back, and when to sign — a post, a

A company can make the manifesto its own: its values held across

it. From there it travels outwards in both directions: delivered to
customers as a seal on what the company hands over, and required of
distributors for what the company receives.

# Framework

## Introduction

The manifesto says what we stand for; the certificate says what a
person promises; the framework is how the promise becomes possible.

Talaia does not replace the way you already work; it sits underneath
it. Stories can live in Jira, GitHub Issues or a text file; a report
can still be a Word document; a post is still a post. What talaia
adds is a boundary between what may be delegated and what must remain
human:

- **The instrument may** draft, build, test, iterate and report.
- **The instrument may not** design the system, decide what counts as
  done, accept its own work, sign it, or put it into the world. Those
  acts are yours.

**Working output is not accepted work.**

## The framework

Like Scrum or Kanban, talaia is a method before it is a tool. Unlike
them, it works per piece of work, not per sprint, so it runs under
whatever process you already have.

It has five steps. The first is done once per system; the other four
are a loop, run once per piece of work:

```
DESIGN ───▶ ( BRIEF ───▶ BUILD ────▶ WATCH ───▶ SIGN )
person        person     instrument  person     person, in the work itself
```

| Step | Who | What happens | Done when |
|------|-----|--------------|-----------|
| **Design** | You | You write what the system is: architecture, conventions, constraints. | It is written and you approve it. |
| **Brief** | You, with the instrument | You say what must be true; the instrument asks about what is loose. | No question is left open. |
| **Build** | The instrument | It makes what was briefed, and shows the evidence. | The Brief is true and the evidence is shown. |
| **Watch** | You | You read what came back and keep only what you approve. | You can explain everything you kept. |
| **Sign** | You | You add the line: the address and your name. | The work carries your name. |

Work with an AI is a conversation: something is asked, something
comes back, and the next turn corrects the last. The loop keeps that
shape: Brief, Build and Watch turn until you are satisfied, and the
Sign closes it. You direct at the start and answer at the end.

None of it is new ceremony. The Design is what you already know about
your system, written down; the Brief is the asking itself; Build is
the instrument working inside the rules; the Watch is you reading
what came back; the Sign is the line you add. Together the steps draw
a single line: **execution may be delegated; judgment may not.**

### Design — the human says what the system is

Nothing is asked yet. The Design is what is true of the system,
written down by you before the first Brief:

1. **The architecture.** What the system is, what it is made of, and
   the principles it keeps.
2. **The conventions.** What "done right" looks like here: how the
   work is made, checked and written.
3. **The constraints.** The hard limits: what may not be done even if
   it works, and the choices that bind what comes after.

Every kind of work has all three: a codebase; a publication — its
line, its house style, what it never prints; a series of reports.

Three rules keep it yours:

- **The instrument does not design the system.** It may help you word
  the Design and format it; it never changes it on its own.
- **Every Brief is read against the Design.** If an ask contradicts
  it, or the work shows it wrong or incomplete, the instrument says
  so and stops.
- **Only you reopen it.** The instrument goes on when you have
  decided: by rewriting the Design yourself, or by dictating the
  change.

**Done when:** the Design is written and you approve it. Until then,
no Brief starts. Signing it as you sign any other work — the line,
your name — is good practice; nothing checks it.

### Brief — the human says what "done" means

Nothing is produced yet. The Brief is not a meeting and not a form:
it is your ask, in your own words, and the conversation that follows
it.

1. **You say what you want**: what must be true when the work is
   finished.
2. **The instrument reads the ask against the Design and the
   project** — the code, the sources it is working in — and returns
   what is loose: a contradiction, an open question, a decision the
   ask leaves to be taken.
3. **You answer.** The instrument takes none of those decisions
   itself: nothing of design, architecture or higher order, and no
   substantial change — a library swapped, a structure reshaped, a
   capability added or removed.

**Done when:** no question is left open. While one remains, the Brief
turns. When none is left, the instrument starts Build on its own,
without asking permission: the ask, what "done" means and every
decision are already yours.

Keep your brief. It is your evidence for clause (a) of the
certificate — the AI helped, and the ask was yours — and the one part
of the work that was yours from the first word. Where it helps,
publish it beside the result.

### Build — the instrument works

Nothing is decided here. The instrument works to the Brief, under
seven rules:

1. **The smallest thing.** The least that makes the Brief true.
2. **Nothing more, nothing less than briefed.** No features,
   abstractions or "improvements" nobody asked for; nothing that was
   asked for silently dropped.
3. **What a person wrote stays as they wrote it.** Hand-made content
   is not touched — above all your edit to something the instrument
   produced. That edit is a decision, and the next turn works around
   it, not over it. Only an explicit ask reopens it.
4. **Proof first, and it never bends.** Where the work can be tested,
   the failing test is written before the work and shown failing;
   where it cannot, the check is named before the work starts. Tests
   and checks are never deleted, skipped or weakened.
5. **Every claim comes with its evidence.** "Tests pass" counts only
   as the real output of tests that ran, shown. "According to the
   data" counts only with the source beside it.
6. **The work stays watchable.** Small enough to be read in full in
   one sitting, coherent enough to be explained without help. When it
   grows past that, it is split.
7. **Iterate, never insist.** If the same failure survives two
   attempts, the instrument stops and returns the decision to you.

**Done when:** what was briefed is true and its evidence is shown —
or two attempts have failed and the decision is back with you.

### Watch — the human keeps guard

The Watch happens where the work already is: the diff in your
editor, the document on your screen, the draft in the chat.

1. **Read every part you are about to accept.** If you cannot explain
   a hunk, a paragraph, a figure, do not accept it: ask, or take it
   over by hand.
2. **Check the evidence, not the claim.** The tests ran in front of
   you, real output included; the sources open; the numbers trace.
   Anything still marked *unverified* is verified by you or removed.
3. **Keep only what you approve.** In code, hunk by hunk, staging the
   ones that pass; in a document, paragraph by paragraph, leaving the
   rest out.
4. **Send back the rest.** All of it or a part: it goes round the
   loop again until it is what you asked for.

Tools may scale the reading, never the judgment. Semantic diffs,
review assistants, summarizers: use whatever helps you read; the
decision to accept stays yours.

**Done when:** you have read everything you kept, you can explain it,
and nothing in it is still unverified.

### Sign — the human answers for it

You add the line — the address and your name — in the work itself;
in software, the commit.

**The instrument does not sign, does not publish, does not write
history.** In software: no `add`, no `commit`, no `push`, no
`merge` — no state-changing git command, ever. Reading history is
allowed.

**Done when:** the work carries your name. The instrument iterates;
you decide when the loop ends, and the signature is that decision.

## Assurance

Anyone can add the line without having read a word of the Build. No
machine will catch it, and the framework does not pretend one will.
What the line does is change what the work *is*: you declare that an
AI helped and that you read the result, understood it and answer for
it, and whoever receives the work reads it as yours — not the
machine's, not a draft, not a proposal — and judges it as your work
is judged.

So assurance comes from the signer. The line does not check the
work; it makes the work yours, and what carries your name you do
properly, because your name is what people will ask for when it
fails. **The certificate does not add a control. It removes an
excuse.**

The receiver's part is to trust the signature, and to ask;
responsibility has always meant asking the one who signed. Why this
and not that; where the figure comes from; what this part does; what
would break it. Whoever read the work and made it their own can
answer without the tool. Whoever signed without reading finds out
what the signature meant. Four things make the asking possible:

- **A name.** The certificate line: the address, and a person who
  answers.
- **The evidence.** The checks the work cites ran, and their output
  is beside it.
- **The size.** The work stays within what one reading can hold.
- **The reading.** Rework and reverts — never throughput — tell
  whether the Watch was real.

# In practice
## The instrument's rules

The framework is applied in two places, and nowhere else. The first
is the person's own discipline: write the design and the brief in
your words, read the work in full, sign only what you can answer
for. No tool
enforces that part; it is the habit the certificate names. The
second is the instrument: the rules — the Design it reads and never
changes on its own, Brief, Build, the refusal to sign — written into the instructions the agent reads on
every turn. Every agent takes such instructions, under one name or
another: `AGENTS.md`, `CLAUDE.md`, a system prompt, a custom
instructions field. The rules go there once, and the agent works
inside them from then on.

> [talaia.dev/agentsmd](https://talaia.dev/agentsmd)

This is the one file that matters: the framework, as the agent reads
it, kept in one place so that every copy says the same thing. Save it
at the root of your repository. Where each tool reads it — Claude
Code, Codex, Gemini, Cursor, Copilot, or a chat window — is at
[talaia.dev/how-to-agentsmd](https://talaia.dev/how-to-agentsmd).

is the document on your screen: you read the draft and keep the

## Software

Software is the medium where the framework maps onto gestures you
already make, and where the rules are strictest: **the instrument
does not write git.** No `add`, no `commit`, no `push`, no `merge`
— nothing enters history without a human decision.

The Design is three files in `.talaia/`, at the root of the
repository — `architecture.md`, `conventions.md`, `constraints.md` —
written by you, read by the agent on every turn, and committed and
signed like any other change. The agent writes in them only at your
dictation.

Brief and Build are the conversation with the agent, under the
rules in its instructions file. Watch and Sign are git:

- **Watch is the diff and the stage.** You read the change in your
  editor, hunk by hunk, and stage what you accept: staging is the
  first act of acceptance.
- **Sign is the commit.** You write the message in your own words
  — the agent never drafts it; if you cannot write the message, you
  cannot commit — with the address in it, and `-s` writes your name
  as the trailer:

  ```sh
  git commit -s -m "feat: export orders as CSV (#42)" -m "talaia.dev/certificate"
  ```

  ```text
  feat: export orders as CSV (#42)

  talaia.dev/certificate

  Signed-off-by: John Doe <john@example.com>
  ```

- **The pull request is the next hand.** The reviewer reads the
  change and puts their own sign-off behind yours; the pull request
  template asks the certificate again, in front of the team, where
  review already happens.

The agent ends every finished piece of work with the certificate and
the command to sign. Same trailer, same gesture, same enforcement as
the DCO: if your project already uses it, one sign-off certifies
both — talaia adds understanding on top of origin, never replacing
it. 

The forge can hold the gate. A check runs on every pull request and
fails when a commit in it carries no `Signed-off-by:`; make it a
required check and no signature means no merge — the DCO's lever,
one level up.

> [talaia.dev/pr-check](https://talaia.dev/pr-check)

One file. Where to put it on GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket or Gitea, and
how to make it required, is at
[talaia.dev/how-to-pr-check](https://talaia.dev/how-to-pr-check).

# Using talaia

This part is for software only: it applies when the work lives in a
git repository. Everything before it holds for any work done with AI.

## Quick start

Install once, then two commands inside your own repository:

```sh
go install talaia.dev/cmd/talaia@latest   # once; Go 1.23+ is the only requirement

cd ~/your-project
talaia init                 # the instructions file, the design files, the pull request template, the check
talaia hooks claude         # optional: deny git writes in the agent's own settings (claude, gemini, git)
talaia audit --strict       # any time: are the commits signed? — read from git
```

Write the design in `.talaia/`, commit the scaffold, open your AI
tool, and work as usual. The CLI
executes no AI and makes no network calls; it creates files, edits
only the settings list that `hooks` owns, and reads git history.
`talaia help` lists the commands.

## talaia init

Run it at the root of your repository. It creates the harness — eight
files — and touches nothing else:

```
AGENTS.md                     The framework rules for AI agents
CLAUDE.md, GEMINI.md          Redirects to AGENTS.md (Claude Code, Gemini CLI)
.github/
├── PULL_REQUEST_TEMPLATE.md  What, why, how it was tested + the certificate
└── workflows/talaia.yml      The audit check on every pull request
.talaia/                      The design: yours to write
├── architecture.md           Overview, structure, principles
├── conventions.md            Coding and development conventions
└── constraints.md            Hard limits and binding decisions
```

| | |
|---|---|
| **The rules** — `AGENTS.md` | The file at [talaia.dev/agentsmd](https://talaia.dev/agentsmd), as it is; yours to adapt. `CLAUDE.md` and `GEMINI.md` are one-line imports of it — put no rules in them. Where every other tool reads it: [talaia.dev/how-to-agentsmd](https://talaia.dev/how-to-agentsmd). |
| **The design** — `.talaia/` | The truths of the system: architecture, conventions, constraints. Seeded with `TODO` placeholders for you to write, before the first Brief. The agent reads them on every turn and never changes them on its own: it stops and asks, and writes there only at your dictation. |
| **The pull request template** | Applied by the forge to every pull request: what changed, why, how it was tested — and the two certificate clauses as checkboxes, ticked in front of the team. |
| **The check** — `.github/workflows/talaia.yml` | The file at [talaia.dev/pr-check](https://talaia.dev/pr-check), as it is: `talaia audit --strict` over the pull request's commits. Make it required and no signature means no merge: [talaia.dev/how-to-pr-check](https://talaia.dev/how-to-pr-check). |

**`init` is idempotent and never destructive.** Existing files are left
exactly as they are; only missing files are created.

## talaia hooks

`AGENTS.md` tells the agent never to run a state-changing git command.
`talaia hooks <agent>` makes that rule mechanical, in the place each
agent obeys, so the commit stays the person's even when the agent
forgets:

```sh
talaia hooks claude   # .claude/settings.json  → permissions.deny: one Bash rule per git write
talaia hooks gemini   # .gemini/settings.json  → tools.exclude: one run_shell_command per git write
talaia hooks git      # .git/hooks/            → reference-transaction + pre-push, this clone only
```

The git writes denied are the ones `AGENTS.md` names and their
equivalents: `add`, `am`, `apply`, `branch`, `checkout`, `cherry-pick`,
`clean`, `commit`, `merge`, `mv`, `pull`, `push`, `rebase`, `reset`,
`restore`, `revert`, `rm`, `stash`, `switch`, `tag`. Read-only git
stays open.

- **`claude` and `gemini`** edit a versioned settings file, so the
  rule reaches everyone who clones. Existing settings are kept: only
  the deny list grows, with what is missing; a file that is not JSON
  is reported and left alone. Running it twice changes nothing.
- **`git`** works with any agent, because every agent ends up calling
  `git`. The two hooks refuse when the process carries an agent's
  environment variable — `CLAUDECODE` (Claude Code), `GEMINI_CLI`,
  `CODEX_SANDBOX`, or `TALAIA_AGENT=1` for any agent you launch
  yourself — and do nothing otherwise, so the person and their tools
  never notice them. `reference-transaction` stops every ref update
  (commit, branch, tag, stash, merge, rebase) even under
  `--no-verify`; `pre-push` stops pushes, unless `--no-verify`. They
  do not cover the index or the working tree (`add`, `reset --hard`,
  `checkout -- file`, `clean`): that is what the agent's own settings
  are for. Hooks live in `.git/hooks/`, so they are per clone: run it
  again after every fresh clone. An existing hook is never
  overwritten; chain them by hand.

None of this is a security boundary. An agent can call git another
way or unset its own variable; a deny rule stops the command the
agent usually writes, not the program. What holds against a
deliberate bypass is that the commit carries the person's name.
Agents with no per-command deny list in the repository — Codex,
Cursor, Copilot, Windsurf, Aider — keep the rule from `AGENTS.md`
alone, plus the git hooks where they set a variable.

## talaia audit

Assurance is a by-product of git, not a report. `talaia audit` reads
the history — one `git log`, read-only, merge commits skipped — and
says whether the signatures are there: how many commits carry
`Signed-off-by:` and a story id, and which do not. With `--strict` it
exits with status 1 on any unsigned commit — a missing story id or an
oversize commit is only reported; that is what the pull request check
runs. Narrow it with whatever you would pass to git log:
`talaia audit main..feature`, `talaia audit --since=2026-08-01`.

## The CLI

Go 1.23+, standard library only. The usual loop:

```sh
make build   # → bin/talaia
make test    # go test ./...
make vet     # go vet ./...
make fmt     # gofmt
```

To get a system-wide binary from a clone, build as your own user
first, so `bin/` is not left owned by root:

```sh
git clone https://github.com/talaia-dev/talaia.git
cd talaia
make build && sudo make install   # → /usr/local/bin/talaia
# or, per user, without sudo:
make install-user                 # → $(go env GOPATH)/bin/talaia
```

Each capability is a self-contained module under
`internal/modules/<name>/` exposing `Register(app *cli.App)`, wired in
`cmd/talaia/main.go`; a new command is a new module.  

## The website

`web/` is an Astro site that renders this README — `web/src/content/docs`
is a symlink to `docs/`, so both paths are literally the same file:
edit either, never replace the link with a copy — and serves it as plain
text at `/llms.txt`. Node 22 (`.nvmrc`):

```sh
cd web && nvm use
npm run dev             # http://localhost:4321
npm run build           # → dist/
npm run check           # verifies the in-text wordmark transform against dist/
sh scripts/og.sh        # re-renders public/og.png (needs headless Chrome)
python3 scripts/icons.py   # regenerates favicon.ico and apple-touch-icon.png
```

The README stays plain markdown; what looks designed on the site — the
wordmark in running text, the two towers, the manifesto pairs, the
cards — is applied at build time from the markdown's own shape (two
small Sätteri plugins and a few CSS selectors). If you rename a
section, the index follows; the one thing that will not is the *Get
started* button, which points at `#the-instruments-rules`.

## How to contribute

talaia.dev grows in two directions, and both are open at
[github.com/talaia-dev/talaia](https://github.com/talaia-dev/talaia):

- **The manifesto and the method** — the ideas. If the interpretation
  misses something, a value pair is wrong, or the loop doesn't survive
  contact with your team, open a GitHub issue and make the case.
  Changes to the manifesto are held to a high bar, measured against the
  foundation, and discussed in the open before any wording changes — it
  is meant to be stable, not static.
- **The techniques and the code** — the practice. Rules for
  `AGENTS.md`, prompts, CI recipes, checks: anything that makes an AI
  agent obey the manifesto in a real project. And the CLI
  itself. This is where most contributions belong, and where the
  framework grows from practice. Every design choice reflects a vision
  of the person who will work inside it; contribute accordingly.

Nothing lands on `main` directly — not from a contributor, not from a
maintainer. Every change is proposed on its own branch and enters the
repository through a pull request, or not at all:

1. **Propose first.** For anything beyond a small fix, open an issue
   and make the case, so the direction is agreed before the work. The
   issue is the story the commits will reference.
2. **Branch, never `main`.** Contributors fork and branch; maintainers
   branch. One change per branch, small enough to read in a sitting.
3. **Verify before proposing**: `make test` and `make vet` for code;
   for docs and techniques, check them against a real project.
4. **Own what you propose.** Talaia is built with its own framework —
   this repository has the `AGENTS.md`, the design files and the
   pull request template that `talaia init` gives yours. An AI may
   write your contribution, but you read the diff before opening the
   pull request, you sign every commit (`git commit -s`, with
   `Assisted-by:` when it applies), and the pull request carries your
   name. The manifesto applies to contributing too.
5. **The pull request is the gate.** `main` is protected: it takes no
   pushes, only merged pull requests, and a pull request merges only
   when its checks pass — the build and tests, and `talaia audit`
   over its own commits: unsigned, no merge — and a
   maintainer has read it and approved. Approval is the reviewer's
   signature: the second hand of the chain.
